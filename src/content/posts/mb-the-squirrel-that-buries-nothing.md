# A Perfect Burial, Minus the Nut

A grey squirrel trots across the grass, stops, and starts to dig. It leans in, presses something into the hole, rakes the soil back, pats it flat, and moves on.

Play the scene back in slow motion and you notice the problem: its paws were empty the whole time. There was no nut. The entire burial was a performance.

## The Study That Caught Them Acting

Michael Steele of Wilkes University and his colleagues spent seasons watching more than a hundred wild grey squirrels handle acorns. Mixed in with the genuine burials, the squirrels kept staging empty ones — digging the hole, miming the deposit, covering it carefully.

At the two study sites, these fake caches made up 13 and 22 percent of all burials — roughly one in five. The behavior even has a formal name now: deceptive caching.

## Why Lie to Your Neighbors?

Because the neighbors are professional burglars. A squirrel can lose as much as a quarter of everything it hides to thieving members of its own species, who watch from branches and fences, note where a burial happens, and dig it up at leisure.

Every fake grave poisons that surveillance. A thief that excavates two or three empty holes stops trusting what it saw, and the real caches — buried elsewhere, often in harder-to-raid spots — survive.

## The Experiment's Twist: Robbery Makes Them Better Liars

Steele's team then did something slightly cruel and very informative: they started stealing the squirrels' caches themselves.

The squirrels responded like security consultants after a break-in. They doubled the rate of fake burials, moved real food into locations that were harder to raid, and ate more of it on the spot rather than storing it at all. The deception was not a fixed habit — it scaled with the actual level of crime in the neighborhood.

## Tactical Deception, Pocket-Sized

Deliberately planting false information for an audience is a sophisticated trick — the kind of thing usually discussed in primate research. Yet here it is in a backyard rodent with a brain the size of a walnut, running counter-surveillance operations against its own kind.

So the next time you see a squirrel bury something, remember: there is a real chance you just watched a lie. The squirrel buries nothing, and the thief digs up nothing.
