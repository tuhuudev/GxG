# The Predator Waiting at the Feeder

A praying mantis is a garden ornament of an insect — a slow, swaying stick that sits politely on a leaf. That reputation is a disguise. Mantises catch, kill, and eat **birds**, and it happens far more often than almost anyone realizes.

## 147 Documented Kills, on Six Continents

In 2017, zoologist Martin Nyffeler and two American co-authors combed through every available report of mantises capturing birds. They found **147 documented cases across 13 countries, on every continent except Antarctica**, involving some **24 different bird species**. Fewer than a third of the cases had ever been published before — the rest had been sitting in scattered notes and photographs.

More than 70% of the reports came from the United States, and the victims were overwhelmingly **hummingbirds ambushed at garden feeders and flowers**, in plain daylight.

## How a Stick Beats a Wing

The mantis does not chase. It waits, motionless, until the bird hovers within reach — then its spiked forelegs snap shut faster than the bird can react. The spikes lock the wings in place, and a hummingbird that weighs about as much as the mantis itself has no leverage left to break free.

Then comes the detail the researchers kept encountering: about two-thirds of the birds were bitten in the head, neck, or throat, and in several cases the mantis had **chewed a hole in the skull and was extracting the brain** while the prey still struggled.

## No Venom, No Web — Just Patience

The mantis brings no toxin and no trap to this fight. Two spiked arms, a fast strike, and the willingness to wait all afternoon at your feeder. The next time one is sitting there, still as a leaf, it may not be decoration.
