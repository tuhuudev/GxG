# The Highest Blood Pressure on Earth

A giraffe lives with a problem no other animal faces at this scale. Its brain sits up to five meters above its heart, and pushing blood that far uphill requires the highest blood pressure known in any animal — roughly 280/180 mmHg, about twice that of a healthy human.

That pressure is survivable as long as the head stays up. The trouble starts at the waterhole.

## Five Meters of Falling Blood

To drink, a giraffe splays its front legs and swings its head down by three to five meters in a single motion. In hemodynamic terms, that is a catastrophe waiting to happen: a tall column of blood, already under double-human pressure, suddenly flowing downhill into the skull.

In a human head, a surge like that would tear blood vessels open. A giraffe does it calmly, several times a day, and walks away every time.

## The Wonderful Net

The first line of defense sits at the base of the skull: the *rete mirabile* — Latin for "wonderful net." It is a dense web of arteries and veins that catches the incoming rush of blood and buffers it, soaking up the pressure spike like a sponge before it can reach the brain.

## Valves That Slam Shut

The second line runs along the neck itself. The jugular veins carry one-way valves that snap closed when the head goes down, stopping blood from flowing backward into the head and pooling there. Between the net below the brain and the valves in the neck, the surge is broken into something the brain never feels.

## An Engineering Problem, Solved in Flesh

Every sip a giraffe takes rides a pressure wave that would drop a person. The animal never notices — because its body was built around the problem long before engineers had a name for it.
