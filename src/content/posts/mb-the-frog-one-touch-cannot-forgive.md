# Smaller Than Your Thumb, Deadlier Than Any Snake

The golden poison frog of Colombia's rainforest is about the size of a paperclip. It does not bite, sting, or chase. It just sits there, brilliant yellow — and touching it can be enough to stop a human heart.

## A Poison That Jams the Body's Wiring Open

The frog's skin is coated in **batrachotoxin**, one of the most potent natural toxins known. Batrachotoxin binds to the sodium channels that nerves and muscles use to fire, and **locks them open**. Sodium floods the cells and never stops; nerves lose the ability to reset and fire again.

The practical result: muscles seize and cannot release, and the heart — a muscle that survives on rhythm — locks up and fails. There is **no antidote**.

A single adult frog carries roughly **1 milligram** of the stuff: enough, by standard estimates, to kill **10 to 20 people**.

## The Frog Cannot Make Its Own Weapon

Here is the twist that took scientists years to untangle: the golden poison frog **cannot synthesize batrachotoxin at all**. It harvests the raw alkaloids from its diet — the mites, ants, and beetles of its patch of rainforest floor — then concentrates them in glands in its skin.

Take away the diet and you take away the weapon. **Frogs raised in captivity on crickets and fruit flies never become toxic**, and wild-caught frogs slowly lose their poison once removed from the forest. The same species that can stop your heart in Colombia is, in a lab terrarium, completely harmless to handle.

## A Warning Label That Means It

In the wild, the frog's blazing color is an honest advertisement — the textbook case of a creature so dangerous it never needs to hide. One touch is all it takes, and every predator in the forest has learned to read the label.
