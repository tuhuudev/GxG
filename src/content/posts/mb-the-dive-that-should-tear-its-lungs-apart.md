# The Fastest Animal Alive Has a Breathing Problem

A peregrine falcon in a hunting dive reaches speeds around **240 miles per hour** — the fastest of any animal on Earth. At that speed, the air slamming into its face is no longer just wind. The pressure should overwhelm its airway and damage its respiratory tract before the falcon ever reaches its prey.

It doesn't. And the reason sits in a place you would never think to look.

## A Bone Cone Inside the Nose

Inside each nostril, the peregrine has a small, cone-shaped bony structure called a **tubercle**. As air rams in at diving speed, the cone breaks the incoming flow into controlled turbulence — slowing it and managing the pressure just enough that the bird can keep drawing oxygen mid-dive instead of having its airway blasted shut.

Engineers have long noted the resemblance between these nasal cones and the inlet cones used in jet engines to manage extreme airflow. The falcon's version came first, by a few million years.

## Twenty-Five Times Gravity

The dive is only half the problem. Pulling out of it is the other half. Leveling off at the bottom of a stoop can subject a falcon to forces of up to **25 G**. For comparison: trained fighter pilots, in pressure suits, typically black out around 8–9 G.

The falcon takes nearly three times that — and stays awake, eyes locked on its target.

## Built for a Speed That Should Kill It

Every part of the peregrine's high-speed toolkit solves a problem that speed itself creates: the nasal cones manage the air, a protective third eyelid shields its vision, and its streamlined body keeps the dive stable. It rides air that would tear an unprotected body apart — and does it every day, on purpose, for lunch.
