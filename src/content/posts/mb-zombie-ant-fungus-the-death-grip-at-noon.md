In the rainforests of Thailand, a fungus called *Ophiocordyceps unilateralis* infects carpenter ants. It grows through the ant's body from the inside, and while it does, it keeps the ant walking — to one very particular place.

## A drunken walk away from the trail

Healthy carpenter ants travel along well-defined trails. Infected ants abandon them. They wander alone over low vegetation in a zigzag, drunken walk, sometimes falling and convulsing.

## A low leaf, close to noon

The infected ants end up on small plants close to the forest floor, usually less than 50 centimetres above the soil. Researchers who watched infected ants found that their final bite happened at around solar noon, suggesting the timing is synchronised with the sun or with temperature and humidity.

## The death grip

The ant clamps its jaws onto a vein on the underside of a leaf. Biting a leaf vein is not normal ant behaviour and has no food value. The fungus then causes the muscles that open and close the mandibles to break down, so the jaws stay locked and the ant cannot let go — even after it dies.

## Dead at sunset, a stalk from the head

The ant does not die at noon. It dies around sunset, still hanging under the leaf. Over the next two to three days the fungus grows out of the body, and a stalk erupts from the back of the ant's head. From there it releases spores that fall onto the forest floor below, where more ants forage.

## The takeaway

The fungus ate the ant from the inside. The ant still walked to the exact spot the fungus needed.
