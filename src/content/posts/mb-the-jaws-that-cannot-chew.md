# The Death Roll: How a Crocodile Eats What It Cannot Chew

Every year, more than a million wildebeest cross the Mara River during the Great Migration, and Nile crocodiles wait for them. The crocodile's bite is famous — thousands of pounds of clamping force. What is less known is that all that force cannot cut anything. Crocodiles do not chew.

## Teeth That Hold but Cannot Cut

Crocodilian teeth are conical pegs, built to grip and puncture rather than slice. A crocodile can swallow small prey whole, but a wildebeest is far too large. To break it into pieces, it needs a different tool.

## The Death Roll

That tool is the death roll. Once the jaws are locked onto a limb, the crocodile presses its legs against its body, cants its head and tail, and spins around its own long axis. Researchers who filmed alligators performing the manoeuvre measured the rotation at about 1.6 revolutions per second — nearly twice a second — and showed that it works purely through conservation of angular momentum. The twisting force tears the held limb away from the body. A 2019 survey found the behaviour in almost every living crocodilian species.

## The River Kills More Than the Crocodiles

Here is the part that surprises people. Research from Yale on the Mara crossings found that crocodiles account for only about 2 percent of the wildebeest that die in the river — roughly one in fifty. The rest drown in the stampede. Around 6,250 carcasses, more than 1,000 tons of biomass, enter the Mara River each year, feeding everything from crocodiles and vultures to the fish and microbes downstream.

## A Predator Built Around a Limitation

The Nile crocodile's most theatrical behaviour exists because of something it cannot do. A bite that cannot cut becomes lethal only when paired with a spin — and even then, the river itself does most of the killing.
