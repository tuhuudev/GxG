# Eighteen Chances

The lionfish is one of the most photographed animals on a reef, and one of the most painful to touch. Brush a hand against it and a spine drives venom into you — and the pain does not peak on contact. It climbs, for hours.

## Eighteen Loaded Spines

A lionfish has eighteen venomous spines: thirteen along the dorsal fin, three on the anal fin and two on the pelvic fins. Each spine is solid with a three-lobed cross-section, and along its upper two-thirds run two grooves packed with venom-producing glandular tissue, covered by a thin membrane. There is no pump. When the spine penetrates skin, the membrane tears and the venom is pressed into the wound.

## What The Venom Does

The venom is a mix of heat-sensitive proteins. Symptoms appear within minutes to a few hours: the sting site swells, turns red and warm, and the pain spreads. Victims report sweating, tingling and muscle weakness as the swelling moves from the hand up the arm. In a study of 117 people stung by lionfish, every one of them — 100% — reported marked pain, frequently described as excruciating, along with local swelling.

## Days, Not Minutes

The pain itself usually lasts several hours. Swelling typically settles over two to three days and bruising can persist for around five, but in severe cases symptoms have been reported to last from eight hours up to a month. Hot — not scalding — water immersion for 30 to 90 minutes is the standard first aid, because heat breaks down the venom proteins.

## Still Dangerous When Dead

The venom does not die with the fish. Scorpionfish relatives including lionfish remain venomous for up to 48 hours after death, which is why divers who spear lionfish handle the carcasses with the same caution as a live one.

## The Loop

One spine, eighteen chances, and a pain that owns your hand for hours — from an animal that never has to attack you to hurt you.
