When a honeybee stings you, the sting does not end when the bee flies off. The stinger stays behind — and keeps working.

## Barbs like a saw

A honeybee's stinger has saw-toothed barbs. Once it is in skin, those barbs anchor it in place.

## The stinger tears out

When the bee pulls away, it cannot take the stinger with it. The stinger and venom sac rip out of the bee's body, and the bee dies. Wasps and hornets, by contrast, can sting repeatedly.

## A pump with no bee attached

The venom sac stays attached to the stinger and acts like a small piston, continuing to push venom into the wound. According to Cleveland Clinic, it can keep pumping for about a minute. In a 1996 study published in *The Lancet*, the welt grew larger the longer the stinger stayed in — even over a few seconds — which is why removing it quickly matters more than how you remove it.

## The banana alarm

The sting site also carries alarm pheromones, chiefly isopentyl acetate, which smells like bananas. The scent excites other worker bees and leads them to sting close to the same spot.

## The takeaway

The bee dies, but its stinger keeps pumping — and calls for backup. If you are stung, get the stinger out fast.
