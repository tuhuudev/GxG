# Not A Bite. An Installation.

A mosquito is in and out in seconds. A tick moves in.

Its feeding tube, the **hypostome**, is lined with rows of backward-facing barbs. Once it is pushed through the skin, those barbs act like tiny anchors: pulling on the tick only drives them in deeper.

## The Cement

Many hard ticks then secrete a glue. Proteins in the saliva harden around the mouthparts into a **cement cone** that locks the tick to the wound. The cone is part of why a tick that has fed for a day or two is so hard to remove cleanly — and why the mouthparts can stay behind when the body is torn off.

## The Numbing

You do not feel any of this because the same saliva carries anaesthetic compounds that dull the skin around the bite. It also carries anticoagulants to keep your blood flowing and immune suppressants to stop your body from noticing the intruder.

## Ten Days

Depending on the species and life stage, a tick stays attached for anywhere from about three to ten days. Over that time its flat body swells with blood into a grey, rounded bead many times its original size. Health agencies warn that disease transmission — Lyme disease, for instance — usually requires the tick to have been attached for more than 24–36 hours, which is exactly why ticks are built to stay.

## The Meat Allergy

Some ticks, including the lone star tick, carry a sugar molecule called **alpha-gal** in their saliva. In some people a bite sensitises the immune system to it — and because the same sugar is found in beef, pork and lamb, they develop **alpha-gal syndrome**: a delayed allergic reaction to red meat that can last for years.

## The Loop

Ten days of barbs cemented into your skin, and you never felt the stab.
