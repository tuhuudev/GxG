Off Seal Island in South Africa, great white sharks hunt Cape fur seals in one of the most dramatic predator–prey contests in the ocean. The shark attacks from far below and can launch its whole body out of the water. And yet a seal that survives the first strike has a surprising place to hide: right next to the shark itself.

## An attack from a hundred feet down

Great whites at Seal Island ambush seals from below. Attacks generally start between about 7 and 31 metres under the surface, most of them close to 30 metres — roughly a hundred feet down. From there the shark accelerates upward at more than 11 metres per second, and it takes only 2 to 2.5 seconds to go from first acceleration to breaching the surface.

Coming from below is no accident. A seal at the surface can only reliably spot a shark about 5 metres (around sixteen feet) beneath it. If it does see the shark coming, it has roughly a tenth of a second to react before contact.

## The seal's counter-moves

Researchers who spent years watching these hunts describe a set of anti-predator tactics used by Cape fur seals:

- **Leaving in groups.** Seals usually leave the island in coordinated groups of 8 to 12 animals, sharing the job of watching for sharks.
- **Zigzagging.** When a single seal or a small group spots a stalking shark below, they perform a tightly controlled zigzag manoeuvre — a tactic sometimes called "working the shark."
- **Riding beside the fin.** When a seal is actively chased, it often swims alongside the shark, around mid-body near the dorsal fin, where the jaws cannot reach it.
- **Scattering.** When a group is hit, the seals burst out of the water in every direction, which likely confuses the shark long enough for some to escape.

## Light decides the hunt

The sharks' success depends heavily on the light. In a study of more than 2,000 natural predation events at Seal Island, success was highest — about 55% — within an hour of sunrise, and dropped quickly as the light increased. Most attacks were over in under a minute, usually after a single breach, and success fell sharply with every extra breach the shark needed.

## The takeaway

A great white can charge from a hundred feet down at full speed. But for a seal that sees it coming, the safest place in the water is beside the shark.
