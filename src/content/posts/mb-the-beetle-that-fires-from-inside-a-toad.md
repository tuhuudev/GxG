# A Chemical Weapon Smaller Than A Coin

The bombardier beetle is a ground beetle you could cover with a coin, and it carries one of the most violent defences in the insect world: a spray of boiling chemicals, fired with an audible pop, that burns skin and stings eyes. Pick one up with bare fingers and you will find out why almost nothing eats it twice.

## Two Chemicals, One Explosion

The beetle stores two ingredients separately inside its abdomen: hydroquinone and hydrogen peroxide. When threatened, it squeezes them into a hardened reaction chamber lined with enzymes. The reaction is violently exothermic — the mixture is converted into irritating benzoquinones, gases build pressure, and the temperature climbs to roughly 100 °C, the boiling point of water.

## Five Hundred Pulses A Second

The spray is not one continuous jet. Valves at the chamber open and close so fast that the beetle can fire in bursts of up to about 500 pulses per second, aiming the nozzle at whatever is attacking it. Each discharge comes with a detonation you can hear, and a single beetle can fire upward of twenty times before its glands run dry.

## What It Does To You

The spray is not lethal to a human, but benzoquinone is highly irritating to skin, eyes and airways. Handling bombardier beetles can leave burns and irritation on the skin, and the chemicals sting the eyes and catch in the throat. For a predator the size of a mouse or a frog, the same blast is a mouthful of boiling, toxic gas.

## Swallowed Whole — And Then The Twist

Toads do not chew. A Japanese common toad simply gulps a beetle down in one movement, which should be the end of the story. In 2018, Shinji Sugiura and Takuya Sato at Kobe University fed bombardier beetles (*Pheropsophus jessoensis*) to two toad species and recorded what happened next. The beetles fired their boiling chemicals from inside the toad's stomach, and 43% of the toads vomited them back up — between 12 and 107 minutes after swallowing them. Every vomited beetle was still alive and active, and one lived on for more than eighteen months afterwards.

## The Loop

Boiling spray on your skin, or boiling spray inside a toad: the bombardier beetle's answer to being attacked is the same, and in almost half the cases, it walks away from the inside of a predator.
