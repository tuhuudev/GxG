# A Turn Past the Breaking Point

An owl can rotate its head about 270 degrees in either direction. A human manages roughly 90 before the neck stops — and for good reason. In our anatomy, forcing a rotation anywhere near an owl's range would stretch and tear the lining of the arteries that feed the brain, the kind of injury that causes strokes.

So the owl's party trick is really a medical puzzle: that much twisting should crush or block the vessels running through its neck. Cut blood to a brain for even a short time and it begins to die. Why doesn't that happen to the owl, dozens of times a day?

## The Johns Hopkins Answer

In 2013, a Johns Hopkins team injected dye into the blood vessels of owls to mimic blood flow, turned the heads, and imaged what happened. What they found was a set of adaptations no one had documented before.

### Blood Reservoirs Under the Jaw

As the owl's vertebral arteries enter the neck, they widen instead of narrowing. Near the base of the head, just under the jaw bone, the vessels balloon into pooling reservoirs. When a spin compresses one route, those pools keep feeding the brain — a built-in reserve tank for exactly the seconds the supply line is pinched.

### A Network With Backup Routes

The owl's vessels are also interconnected, so even if the twist blocks blood along one path, flow continues through another. Human arteries do the opposite — they get smaller under rotation, with no ballooning and no slack to spare.

## Why the Owl Has No Choice

The strangest part is *why* the owl turns at all: its eyes are fixed in their sockets. They cannot swivel the way ours do. To look left, an owl must turn its whole head left. The spin that would kill a person is simply how an owl looks around.
