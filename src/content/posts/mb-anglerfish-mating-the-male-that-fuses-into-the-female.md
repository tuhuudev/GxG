In the pitch-black midnight zone of the ocean, finding a partner is hard. Some deep-sea anglerfish solve that problem in one of the strangest ways in nature: the male bites the female and becomes part of her body.

## A much smaller male

In species that practise this, the males are dwarfs compared with the females — in some, less than a tenth of her length.

## Found by smell, in total darkness

Males track females using chemical signals, with large nostrils built to pick up the scent females release into the dark water.

## A bite that never lets go

When a male reaches a female, he bites into her skin. In permanently attaching species he does not let go again: the tissue at the bite fuses with hers.

## One bloodstream, one body

As the tissues merge, their circulatory systems join, so the female's blood flows through the male and keeps him alive. Yale researchers describe how the male's head dissolves into the female, and he becomes a permanent sperm-producing organ attached to her body. His own eyes and organs waste away.

## An immune system that looks away

A body normally attacks foreign tissue. A 2020 study in *Science* found that these anglerfish have an altered immune response, which is why the female does not reject the fused male. Not every anglerfish does this: in some species the male bites, mates and lets go.

## The takeaway

One bite, and the male melts into the female for the rest of his life. A single female can carry several males attached to her body.
