# The Perfect Burglary

The common vampire bat (*Desmodus rotundus*) commits the same crime every night of its life: it opens a sleeping animal's skin, drinks its blood for twenty to thirty minutes, and leaves without ever being noticed. Every stage of the theft runs on specialized equipment.

## Seeing Heat Through Skin

The bat does not guess where to bite. Heat-sensitive receptors on its nose detect infrared radiation, pointing it to spots where warm blood runs just beneath the surface — a thermal map of its victim's veins.

## A Cut With No Pain

Its teeth are so sharp that the incision is clean enough to go unfelt. The host animal rarely wakes. No struggle, no alarm — just a small, precise opening.

## Spit That Keeps the Tap Open

A wound this small should clot and close in minutes. The bat's saliva prevents that: it contains a potent anticoagulant — researchers named the protein *draculin* — that inhibits the host's clotting factors. The blood keeps flowing, and the bat laps it up like a cat at a bowl; it does not suck. A single session removes around 30 milliliters — a few spoonfuls of its victim.

## The Fragile Vampire

Here is the twist: the thief lives on a knife's edge. A vampire bat that fails to feed for two consecutive nights — roughly 50 to 70 hours — runs out of energy to keep its body warm and starves to death.

The colony's answer is one of the most famous cooperative behaviors in mammals, first documented by zoologist Gerald Wilkinson in 1984: fed bats regurgitate blood to feed starving roost mates, mouth to mouth — and bats that received such a gift are more likely to return it when the donor is starving later.

Then night falls, and the quiet drinking starts again.
