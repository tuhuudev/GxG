# The Undertakers of the Ant Nest

Ant colonies run funerals with industrial efficiency. When a worker dies, other workers pick up the corpse, carry it out of the nest, and dump it on the colony's refuse pile — a kind of communal graveyard biologists call a midden. The behavior even has a scientific name: necrophoresis.

The efficiency hides a strange question. In the dark of a nest, in a crowd of thousands, how does an ant decide that a nestmate is dead?

## One Drop of Death

In 1958, the biologist E. O. Wilson went looking for the answer. His team treated objects — and then live ants — with oleic acid, a compound released by decomposing insects.

The result was absolute. Anything carrying the smell was, in Wilson's words, invariably carried to the refuse pile. Including the living.

A healthy ant daubed with a drop of oleic acid would be seized by her own sisters and hauled, legs kicking, out to the corpse pile. The struggling did not matter. The walking did not matter. She smelled dead, therefore she was dead.

## The Corpse That Kept Coming Home

What happened next is the best part of the experiment. The "dead" ant would pick herself up, clean herself off, and walk back into the nest.

If enough of the acid remained, the undertakers simply grabbed her and dumped her on the pile again — and again — until she had groomed herself clean enough to smell alive. Wilson's conclusion was that ants, with tiny brains and limited processing power, do not weigh up evidence of death. They key on a chemical signal, and the signal overrides everything their other senses tell them.

## The Smell of Being Alive

Fifty years later, researchers at UC Riverside found a second half to the story — and it flips the logic around.

Studying Argentine ants, Dong-Hwan Choe and colleagues showed that living ants are coated in chemicals of their own: dolichodial and iridomyrmecin, which the team called signals of life. As long as those chemicals are present, nestmates read the ant as alive — even if it is motionless.

When the ant dies, those life signals start to evaporate. Within about ten minutes their levels have halved. By around forty minutes, no trace remains — and only then does the corpse become a corpse in the colony's eyes. That explains a puzzle in the older theory: Argentine ants remove their dead far too quickly for slow-building decomposition smells to be the trigger. The funeral does not wait for the smell of death to arrive. It starts the moment the smell of life runs out.

## Death Is a Chemical

Put the two experiments together and you get a complete, slightly unsettling picture of how a colony perceives mortality. An ant is alive as long as it wears the right molecules, and dead the moment it wears the wrong ones. Movement, breathing, protest — none of it counts as testimony.

It is a system with obvious blind spots, and the blind spots are the point. For a superorganism of many thousands of workers, a fast chemical rule that is occasionally wrong beats a slow judgment that is usually right. Corpses carry disease, and a colony that debates each death loses the sanitation race. So the rule stands: one wrong smell, and the living join the graveyard.
